## Reading quiz (7 questions, covers both lessons, inference-based)

1. **Steamboats changed river commerce mainly because they:**
   a) eliminated the need for river ports
   b) could travel upstream against the current quickly, unlike earlier flatboats *(correct)*
   c) were the first boats able to carry passengers
   d) were cheaper to build than flatboats

2. **A Mississippi town's economic success in the 1800s depended heavily on:**
   a) its distance from the state capital
   b) the number of churches it had
   c) whether it sat directly on a river with reliable steamboat access *(correct)*
   d) its elevation above sea level

3. **The Gulf Coast's early economy differed from the river towns' economy mainly because it was built around:**
   a) large cotton plantations
   b) gold mining
   c) wheat farming
   d) shipping, fishing, and trade with other Gulf ports *(correct)*

4. **The "levees only" policy that failed in 1927 assumed that:**
   a) levees alone, without spillways, could fully control the river *(correct)*
   b) no flooding was possible if enough sandbags were used
   c) the river needed to be completely rerouted
   d) only the Delta needed flood protection

5. **One lasting effect of the 1927 flood on Mississippi's population was:**
   a) an increase in the number of counties
   b) the founding of several new river towns
   c) a temporary ban on cotton farming
   d) a wave of migration by Black Mississippians leaving the state *(correct)*

6. **Mississippi's Gulf Coast rebuilt largely in place after Hurricane Camille (1969) mainly because:**
   a) building codes at the time required it
   b) no other land was available near the coast
   c) many at the time treated Camille as unlikely to be repeated *(correct)*
   d) federal law required identical reconstruction

7. **Comparing Camille and Katrina shows that:**
   a) Katrina was a weaker storm that caused more damage by chance
   b) decisions made after one disaster can directly shape the impact of a later one *(correct)*
   c) hurricanes never strike the same stretch of coast twice
   d) building codes have had no real effect on storm damage over time

## Primary Source Exercise 3.1 — Mark Twain, *Life on the Mississippi* (1883)

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** What does the dramatic change Twain describes, from a "dead town" to one "alive and moving," suggest about how much a river town's daily rhythm depended on steamboat traffic?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why would the arrival of a single steamboat cause an entire town's economic activity to burst into motion all at once?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose a town like the one Twain describes had no reliable way to know when a steamboat was arriving. How might that uncertainty have affected the town's ability to do business with it?
4. **(Conceptual)** Twain wrote this passage decades after the events it describes, based on his memory of piloting steamboats before the Civil War. What does knowing this tell you about how to read the passage as a historical source?

## Primary Source Exercise 3.2 — The 1927 Flood Photograph Collection

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** What do the photographs suggest about which kinds of property people prioritized saving during the flood?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why would a railroad company have had a strong practical reason to document the flood's damage so extensively?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose no photographs of the 1927 flood had survived. How would that change what historians could confidently claim about the disaster today?
4. **(Conceptual)** Photographs can show what a scene looked like without explaining who is in it or why. What questions can you not answer just by looking at these images, no matter how closely you study them?

## VR Activity — "Rivers of Commerce, Rivers of Risk"

Platform: ENGAGE.

**Title:** Rivers of Commerce, Rivers of Risk

**What you're doing:** You'll compare a scene of river commerce to a scene of river flooding, using this week's primary sources as your guide, and reflect on how the same geography produced both.

**What you'll need:** VR headset (desktop/mobile fallback available), this week's two lessons and two primary sources already read.

**How to join:** [Session type: temporary] · Session ID: [TBD] · Location: [TBD — a Location depicting a 19th-century river landing or port town; specific Location to be confirmed once available in the ENGAGE library] · Spawn point: at the river landing.

**What to do, in order:**
1. At the landing, place a sticky note describing what Twain's passage suggests the town felt like just before a steamboat arrived.
2. Place a second sticky note describing what one photograph from the 1927 Flood Photograph Collection shows happening in a similar kind of location, once floodwaters arrived instead of a steamboat.
3. Record a short spatial recording (60–90 seconds) explaining one way the river's power to help a town and its power to harm a town come from the exact same geographic fact.

**What to submit:** Your spatial recording, submitted through Canvas.

**Time:** Approximately 20 minutes.

**Facilitator note:** Students sometimes treat commerce and disaster as unrelated topics. The goal of this activity is to help them see both as consequences of the same underlying geography: a river that moves large volumes of water and goods equally well. If you're supporting a live section, ask a student to name one thing a river town could do to benefit from the river without becoming as vulnerable to it.

**Success criteria:** Completion-based. The recording draws a genuine connection between the two primary sources rather than describing them independently.
