In 1724, France issued a single legal code to govern nearly every aspect of enslaved people's lives in its new colony, a code that would shape Mississippi's laws and society for decades after France itself was gone.

📚 PART 1 · LABOR IN A YOUNG COLONY

Building an Economy from Nothing

French Louisiana faced an immediate, practical problem in its earliest years: clearing land, building structures, and growing crops all required enormous amounts of labor, and the colony had far too few French settlers to supply it. France initially relied partly on French indentured laborers, who worked for a fixed period in exchange for passage to the colony, but this source proved insufficient and unreliable, since indentured workers eventually finished their terms and few new laborers volunteered to cross the Atlantic for a struggling, disease-prone colony with an uncertain future and no guarantee of the prosperity recruiters back in France sometimes promised.

Starting in the 1710s, France began importing enslaved Africans directly to Louisiana in significant numbers, a labor system French colonial officials saw as the practical foundation for the colony's economic survival. The Company of the Indies, a French trading company granted a monopoly over Louisiana's commerce for a period in the 1710s and 1720s, organized much of this forced migration directly, treating enslaved human beings as cargo alongside the other goods it shipped and traded as part of its colonial business. Ships carrying enslaved Africans to Louisiana crossed the Atlantic under brutal conditions, and a significant number of captives did not survive the journey at all, a human cost the company's own trading records generally reduced to numbers and cargo losses rather than acknowledged as the deaths of individual people.

This decision had consequences that would outlast French rule in Mississippi by well over a century. The enslaved population, and the plantation-based economy built around their forced labor, expanded steadily through the following decades of French, then British, then Spanish, then American control. The system of slavery you will study in far more detail in Quarter 2 had its origins here, in decisions French colonial administrators made in the first decades of the 1700s to solve an immediate labor shortage, decades before Mississippi existed as a British, Spanish, or American territory at all. Later chapters of this course will trace how this same basic labor system persisted, and in many ways intensified, through each subsequent change of colonial and eventually state government, right up through the antebellum period you will study in the second half of this quarter.

📚 PART 2 · A CODE THAT GOVERNED EVERY DAY LIFE

The Code Noir of 1724

In 1724, France issued a version of its Code Noir, or "Black Code," specifically adapted for Louisiana, based closely on an earlier version France had issued for its Caribbean colonies decades before. The code regulated an enormous range of daily life: it required enslaved people to be instructed in the Catholic faith and baptized, it restricted marriage and gatherings, it set out punishments for a wide range of offenses, and it also, on paper, limited some of the treatment enslavers could legally inflict, though enforcement of those limits was inconsistent at best and depended entirely on local officials who often had every incentive to look away.

The code additionally banned Jewish residents from the colony entirely, a provision with no connection to slavery but a clear reflection of the code's broader purpose: using law to enforce a specific religious and social order the French crown wanted imposed on its colony. Reading the Code Noir as a single document, rather than as a specifically racial law alone, helps explain why: French colonial law in this period treated religious conformity, social order, and the regulation of enslaved labor as connected parts of the same project of building a controlled, orderly colonial society, all administered from a very great distance by a crown that had never seen the colony itself.

The Code Noir's influence did not end when French control of the region did. Spanish and later American authorities in the region drew on many of its underlying assumptions when writing their own slave codes, even after the specific French legal document itself no longer applied. Understanding the Code Noir is not just understanding an isolated French law. It is understanding the legal foundation that later slave codes in Mississippi, including the one you will examine directly in Quarter 2, were built upon.

This kind of legal continuity across changes in colonial rulers was not unusual. Governments that took over a colony from a previous power often found it easier to keep existing legal structures largely in place, adjusting them gradually, than to design an entirely new legal system from scratch. This meant that the specific choices French administrators made in 1724, choices shaped by France's own particular legal traditions and religious priorities at that moment, continued shaping daily life in Mississippi long after those administrators, and the government they served, had left the region entirely.
