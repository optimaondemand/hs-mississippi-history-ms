📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Before Mississippi could become a state, Congress first had to decide what "Mississippi" even was, and that decision required settling a boundary dispute with an entirely different state.
Statehood did not begin with a constitution or a governor. It began with an act of Congress in 1798 that created a new territory out of contested land, borrowed its government structure from an existing model used elsewhere in the country, and made a specific, deliberate choice about slavery that would shape Mississippi's future for decades. This lesson reads that founding act directly.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
territory (TAIR-ih-tor-ee): an organized region of the United States governed directly by the federal government, not yet a state, usually understood as a step on the path toward eventual statehood.
enabling act (en-AY-bling AKT): a law passed by Congress authorizing the people of a territory to write a constitution and form a state government, the formal first step toward admission as a state.
📚 PART 1 · SETTLING A DISPUTE THAT WASN'T ABOUT MISSISSIPPI

A Territory Born from a Boundary Dispute

On April 7, 1798, Congress passed "An Act for an amicable settlement of limits with the state of Georgia, and authorizing the establishment of a government in the Mississippi territory." Notice what that title actually leads with: a boundary dispute with Georgia, not Mississippi itself. Georgia had long claimed vast western lands stretching toward the Mississippi River, overlapping with territory the United States also considered its own, and the same 1798 law that created the Mississippi Territory began by authorizing commissioners to sort out exactly which land belonged to whom.

Once those claims were addressed, the act defined a specific district, bounded by the Mississippi River, the Chattahoochee River, and lines running to the thirty-first degree of north latitude, and declared it "one district, to be called the Mississippi Territory." This was not yet anything close to the modern state's shape or borders, which would take decades and several more acts of Congress to settle, but it was the first time "Mississippi" existed as an organized political unit at all, rather than simply land claimed by competing powers and nations.

The act then borrowed its government structure wholesale from an existing model: the territory would be governed, in the law's own words, "in all respects similar to that now exercised in the territory northwest of the river Ohio," meaning the same basic system Congress had already used to organize what is now Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, and neighboring states under the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. A governor, appointed judges, and an eventual elected assembly as the population grew, all modeled on a system already tested elsewhere, rather than invented from nothing for Mississippi alone.

One detail of that borrowed structure is worth noting specifically. The act combined two jobs into one office: it declared that "the powers, duties and emoluments of a superintendent of Indian affairs for the southern department, shall be united with those of governor." In other words, from the very beginning, Mississippi's territorial governor was simultaneously the federal government's chief official for managing relations with the Choctaw, Chickasaw, and other nations whose land the territory bordered or overlapped. The treaties and land cessions you studied in Week 11 were not handled by some separate, distant federal office. They ran directly through the same office that governed the territory's own settlers day to day.

The boundary dispute with Georgia that opened this lesson did not resolve quickly or cheaply. It would take years of further negotiation, and eventually a payment of one million two hundred fifty thousand dollars to Georgia, before the competing claims this 1798 act first authorized commissioners to examine were fully settled. Statehood, when it finally came, would be built on top of that expensive, hard-won resolution, not around it.

📚 PART 2 · ONE MODEL, ONE DELIBERATE EXCEPTION

A Government Modeled on the Northwest Ordinance, With One Exclusion

The 1798 act's own text is precise about how closely it followed the Northwest Ordinance, and precise about the one place it deliberately did not. The law authorizes a government "similar to that now exercised in the territory northwest of the river Ohio," and then adds a specific phrase: "excepting and excluding the last article of the ordinance made for the government thereof." The Northwest Ordinance's final article was the provision banning slavery in that territory. Congress wrote the Mississippi Territory Act to copy nearly everything else about the Northwest Ordinance's government structure while explicitly leaving that one article out, meaning slavery would remain lawful in Mississippi Territory from its very creation.

This was not an oversight or a vague silence on the subject. The act names the excluded article specifically, which means whoever drafted this law knew exactly what they were choosing to leave out and why. At the same time, the act's seventh section did place one real limit on slavery: it made it illegal to import enslaved people into the territory directly from outside the United States, punishable by a three-hundred-dollar fine per person and freedom for anyone imported in violation of the law. Slavery already established within the United States could continue and expand into the new territory; the international slave trade specifically could not supply it directly.

This distinction, permitting domestic slavery while restricting its direct foreign supply, is easy to read past quickly, but it is a genuinely consequential choice this course will return to directly in Weeks 15 through 17, when Mississippi's plantation economy and the evolution of slavery within the state become the central subject. For now, the point to hold onto is this: Mississippi's founding document as a territory did not leave slavery's legal status to later debate or accident. It settled the question, in specific and deliberate language, from the very first act of Congress that gave Mississippi a government at all.

Nothing about this outcome was strictly guaranteed in advance. Congress had, after all, just banned slavery in the territory northwest of the Ohio River a decade earlier, proving that excluding slavery from a new territory was a real option Congress was willing to exercise elsewhere. Choosing not to exercise that same option for Mississippi reflected specific pressures, the existing presence of slavery among settlers already living in the region under earlier Spanish and British rule, and the economic interests of a growing planter population, not some automatic rule that new Southern territories always had to permit it. A different Congress, facing a differently organized set of pressures, could plausibly have written this act differently.
