📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Both constitutions divide power into three branches and both protect certain rights, yet only one of them writes slavery's protection directly into its own text as a deliberate, standalone article.
Mississippi's constitution did not exist in a vacuum. It was written by people who had the U.S. Constitution, already three decades old by 1817, as a working model in front of them. This lesson compares the two documents directly: where they share a common structure, where they genuinely diverge, and what those differences reveal about the specific priorities of a state constitution versus a federal one.

📚 PART 1 · A SHARED FRAMEWORK, DIFFERENT DETAILS

Same Basic Structure, Different Scale

The two documents share an obvious family resemblance. Both divide government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Both open with a statement of rights, a Declaration of Rights in Mississippi's case and, once added in 1791, a Bill of Rights at the federal level. Both create a chief executive with veto power checked by the legislature, and both establish courts whose judges serve for extended terms rather than facing frequent reelection. None of this resemblance is a coincidence. Mississippi's framers had the federal constitution as a working example already three decades old, and they borrowed its basic architecture deliberately rather than inventing an entirely new system from nothing.

The two documents diverge sharply, however, on how they can be changed. Mississippi's constitution required a two-thirds vote of the General Assembly recommending that voters decide, at the next election, whether to hold a convention to amend or change the constitution, meaning any real revision passed through the state's own elected representatives and then back to the voters directly. The U.S. Constitution's amendment process, by contrast, requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a convention called by two-thirds of the states, followed by ratification from three-fourths of all state legislatures, a considerably higher bar reflecting the federal system's need to secure broad agreement across many separate, independent states rather than within one legislature answering to one electorate.

This difference in amendment difficulty is not a minor technical detail. A state constitution answering to a single legislature and a single body of voters can realistically be changed within a few years if enough political will exists. A federal constitution requiring broad agreement across dozens of separate state governments is deliberately built to resist rapid change, prioritizing stability and durability over responsiveness to any single moment's political mood. Judging one process better than the other misses the point: each was built for the specific kind of government it governs, a single state's legislature in one case, a federation of separate states in the other.

The two rights documents also differ in one telling way. The federal Bill of Rights, added as amendments in 1791, mostly restrains the national government's own power over individuals: it protects speech, religion, and due process against federal interference specifically. Mississippi's Declaration of Rights, written into the constitution's original text rather than added afterward as amendments, restrains Mississippi's own state government just as directly, protecting many of the same basic liberties at the state level from the very moment the document took effect, without needing a later amendment process to secure them. Building a rights protection into the founding text itself, rather than adding it after the fact under political pressure as the federal government eventually did, is its own small sign of how much the convention had already learned from watching the earlier federal example unfold.

📚 PART 2 · TWO DIFFERENT WAYS OF HANDLING SLAVERY

Indirect Compromise Versus Direct Protection

The U.S. Constitution's own treatment of slavery is notoriously indirect. It never uses the word "slave" or "slavery" anywhere in its original text, instead referring obliquely to "other persons" in the three-fifths clause determining how enslaved people counted toward a state's representation in Congress, and to a "person held to service or labour" in the fugitive slave clause. It permitted Congress to ban the international slave trade only after 1808, a deliberately delayed compromise between states wanting the trade to continue and those wanting it stopped. These provisions reflect a document built on compromise language among delegates who could not fully agree with each other, producing careful, euphemistic phrasing rather than a direct, named protection.

Mississippi's constitution, as Lesson 14.1 showed, takes a completely different approach. Article VI names slavery directly and protects it explicitly, barring the legislature from emancipating enslaved people without owner consent and guaranteeing settlers' right to bring enslaved people into the state. There is no euphemism here, no careful indirection. A single state legislature, representing a much narrower and more unified set of interests than the delegates who compromised their way through the federal convention in Philadelphia, could simply write the protection plainly, without needing to paper over disagreement that, within Mississippi itself, did not meaningfully exist among those who held political power.

This contrast is genuinely instructive about how a compromise document differs from a document with a narrower, more unified drafting body behind it. The federal Constitution's indirection reflects real disagreement it had to paper over to get ratified at all. Mississippi's directness reflects the near-absence of that same disagreement among the men who held power at its own constitutional convention. Neither approach is more or less historically significant than the other; each simply reveals something true about the different political communities that produced it, a comparison this course's ongoing arc toward Weeks 15 through 17 will make impossible to set aside.

This week closes the self-government block this course has followed since Week 10: Gálvez's conquest, the Choctaw removal treaties, territorial organization, early political competition, and now a written constitution comparable to the nation's own. Judged across all five weeks together, Mississippi's political community by 1817 had solved several of the first-order problems this course keeps returning to, stable offices, predictable law, orderly transfers of power, while leaving one enormous first-order problem completely unresolved: a legal and economic system built on the permanent, constitutionally protected bondage of a large portion of the very population it governed.
