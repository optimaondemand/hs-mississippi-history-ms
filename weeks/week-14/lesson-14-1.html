📚 PART 1 · STRUCTURING A NEW GOVERNMENT

Seven Articles, One Government

The convention Week 12 described met at Washington, Mississippi, beginning July 7, 1817, and worked for a month and eight days before adopting the finished constitution on August 15. The document organizes Mississippi's government into seven articles: a Declaration of Rights containing twenty-nine separate sections, a short article distributing power among three branches, then dedicated articles for the legislative, executive, and judicial departments, and a final article of general provisions covering everything from the militia to impeachment to slavery to the process for amending the constitution itself.

The Declaration of Rights opens with a direct statement of popular sovereignty: "all political power is inherent in the people, and all free governments are founded on their authority, and instituted for their benefit." This is not a minor flourish. It is the constitution's own explanation of where its authority comes from, echoing language familiar from the U.S. Constitution and the Declaration of Independence before it, and setting up everything that follows as an exercise of power the people themselves are understood to hold and to have delegated, not power the government possesses on its own account.

Article II makes the separation of powers explicit and firm: government is divided into legislative, executive, and judicial departments, and no department may exercise powers belonging to another "except in the instances hereinafter expressly directed." Judged by this course's standard for evaluating self-government, whether a political community builds real structural safeguards against any single office accumulating too much power, this is a genuine, deliberate choice, not an accident of drafting. The convention could have written a much more concentrated government. It chose not to.

The executive and judicial articles fill in the practical detail. The governor served a two-year term, renewable, and held real power within that structure: the authority to veto legislation and to appoint many state officers, checked by the legislature's own ability to override a veto and to confirm or reject appointments. Judges on the state's supreme court and lower courts served during "good behavior," meaning they held office indefinitely unless impeached or removed for misconduct, rather than facing regular reelection, a structure intended to insulate judicial decisions from short-term political pressure.

The Declaration of Rights also specified exactly who could vote, a detail Week 13 already previewed in general terms. Qualified voters had to be free, white, male, at least twenty-one years old, a United States citizen who had lived in the state at least one year and the county at least six months, and either enrolled in the militia or a payer of county or territorial taxes. Every one of these qualifications was a deliberate boundary line, drawn by the convention itself, determining exactly whose political power the Declaration of Rights' opening promise actually extended to in practice, and whose it left out entirely, regardless of how that person's life was otherwise bound up with the state's laws, taxes, and daily governance.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT ARTICLE VI PROTECTED

A Constitution That Entrenched Slavery Deliberately

Article VI's general provisions include specific, direct language protecting slavery. The constitution states plainly that "the General Assembly shall have no power to pass laws for the emancipation of slaves, without the consent of their owners, unless where a slave shall have rendered to the State some distinguished service," a narrow, almost theoretical exception surrounding what was otherwise an outright constitutional ban on the legislature freeing anyone without an owner's consent. The same article further specifies that the legislature "shall have no power to prevent immigrants to this State from bringing with them such persons as are deemed slaves by the laws of any one of the United States," guaranteeing that settlers arriving from other slaveholding states could bring enslaved people with them without interference.

Recall from Week 12 that the 1798 Territory Act had already permitted slavery within Mississippi by deliberately excluding the Northwest Ordinance's anti-slavery article. This constitution goes considerably further. It does not merely permit slavery to exist; it actively restricts the legislature's own future power to limit or end it, protecting slaveholders' interests directly against any future legislative majority that might wish to change course. This is not silence on the subject and not a passive inheritance from the territorial period. It is an affirmative, structural choice, written into the state's founding law by the same convention that also wrote the popular sovereignty language and separation of powers this lesson opened with.

Holding both of these facts in view at once, rather than emphasizing one and minimizing the other, is exactly what this course's approach to Mississippi's history asks of you. The same document that structured a genuinely deliberate, power-dividing government and grounded its authority explicitly in the people also deliberately entrenched slavery beyond ordinary legislative reach. Neither fact cancels the other out. Both are part of an honest account of what Mississippi's first constitution actually did, a tension this course will examine in far greater depth when Weeks 15 through 17 turn directly to the plantation economy and the lived experience of slavery in Mississippi.

Judged by the Classical Republican standard this course has applied throughout, a regime's moral character helps explain, rather than merely decorate, its history, Article VI's protections were not incidental to Mississippi's early prosperity. They were a direct legal foundation for the plantation economy already beginning to take root along the river and in the emerging cotton belt, a foundation later weeks will show carried enormous, lasting costs for the enslaved people it deliberately kept beyond the legislature's reach to free.
