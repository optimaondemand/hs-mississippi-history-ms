📚 PART 1 · THREE DIFFERENT PROJECTS

Different Goals, Different Methods

Spain, France, and England approached Mississippi with genuinely different priorities, and those priorities shaped everything about how each power behaved once it arrived. Spain's De Soto expedition sought quick wealth through conquest, in the pattern of Spain's earlier, spectacular successes against the Aztec and Inca Empires, and left no lasting settlement when that wealth failed to materialize. France sought a sustainable colonial economy built on trade and, eventually, enslaved agricultural labor, prioritizing a functioning colony over quick riches, though French Louisiana grew slowly and struggled financially for decades. England pursued organized settlement through land grants, actively recruiting colonists from its already-established Atlantic seaboard colonies, a strategy that built population more directly than either Spanish conquest or French trade-based colonization had.

These priorities were not arbitrary. Each reflected the specific position and experience of the power pursuing it. Spain already possessed vast, wealthy American territories by the time De Soto arrived, and its colonial playbook, developed against the Aztec and Inca, emphasized rapid conquest of existing wealth over patient settlement. France, with a smaller American colonial presence and less prior experience in the region, built its strategy around trade relationships and a labor-intensive plantation economy instead. England, arriving latest of the three to the Mississippi region specifically, already had decades of experience running settled colonies along the Atlantic coast, and simply extended that established model westward once the opportunity arose.

These different approaches also produced different relationships with Native nations. De Soto's expedition treated Native towns primarily as sources of food and forced labor to be extracted through threat of force. French colonial officials generally sought trade relationships and, at times, military alliances, treating Native nations as partners whose cooperation mattered to French colonial interests, even when that partnership was unequal. British settlement policy, focused on granting land to English-speaking colonists, treated Native land claims as an obstacle to be negotiated around or displaced, a pattern that would intensify dramatically once the United States inherited the same territory.

None of this means any single European power treated Native nations well by any reasonable modern standard. Each approach still ultimately served European interests first, and each contributed, in its own way, to the pressure Native nations faced to cede land, adapt their economies to European trade, or relocate entirely. The differences described here are differences in method and immediate priority, not differences in whether Native sovereignty and land were ultimately respected.

It is worth returning, too, to the contingency emphasized throughout this quarter. None of these three colonial projects succeeded by some predetermined destiny. De Soto's expedition could have found the wealth it sought and returned to establish a genuine Spanish colony. French Louisiana might have grown into a thriving, dominant colony rather than a struggling one, had circumstances in France or Louisiana itself gone differently. British West Florida might have remained British far longer than sixteen years, had the American Revolution and Spain's involvement in it never occurred. Mississippi's colonial history is a record of what actually happened, not a record of what had to happen.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT EACH POWER LEFT BEHIND

Legacies That Outlasted Governments

None of the three European powers you've studied this quarter still governs Mississippi, and yet each left something behind that outlasted its own political control. Spanish exploration left few lasting institutions in Mississippi specifically, but it opened the pattern of European contact that would eventually reshape the entire region, and it briefly returned in force when Spain later governed West Florida after 1779, a period you will encounter again when this course reaches the American Revolution. French colonization left the Code Noir's legal legacy, place names, and a pattern of plantation agriculture built on enslaved labor that would only intensify under later governments. British rule left land grant patterns and an English-speaking settler population whose descendants would go on to seek American statehood for the territory.

This pattern, an institution or population outlasting the government that created it, is one you should watch for throughout the rest of this course. Mississippi's history is not a story of one government cleanly replacing another. It is a story of layers, each new government inheriting, adapting, and building on what came before, whether or not that inheritance was ever formally acknowledged.

Consider, as one final example, the region's population itself by the time of the American Revolution. French, British, and Spanish colonists, alongside enslaved Africans and their descendants, and the Choctaw, Chickasaw, and remaining Natchez, all lived within or near the same relatively small stretch of territory, each community carrying its own language, legal traditions, and religious practices forward from whichever government had brought them there. No single flag, however officially dominant on paper, ever fully erased what the flags before it had already built.
