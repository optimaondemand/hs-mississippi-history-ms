📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1820, a treaty signed at a trading post called Doak's Stand promised to exchange only, in its own words, "a small part" of Choctaw land in Mississippi for land west of the river. Ten years later, a second treaty took the rest.
Removal did not arrive in Mississippi as a single, sudden decision. It arrived in stages, through specific treaties negotiated at specific moments, each shaped by circumstances that could genuinely have gone another way. This lesson reads the actual words of the earlier of those two treaties, the Treaty of Doak's Stand, and asks what its own stated purpose reveals about how removal became total only a decade later, not from the very beginning.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
removal (rih-MOOV-uhl): the United States government's policy, carried out mainly in the 1830s, of forcing Native nations in the Southeast to give up their homelands and relocate west of the Mississippi River.
Trail of Tears (TRAYL uhv TEERZ): the name given to the forced journeys west that Native nations, including the Choctaw, endured under removal, marked by severe hardship and significant loss of life.
📚 PART 1 · A TREATY THAT CLAIMED TO PRESERVE, NOT REMOVE

The Treaty of Doak's Stand, 1820

In October 1820, United States commissioners Andrew Jackson and Thomas Hinds met with Choctaw leaders, including Mingoes Puckshenubbe and Mushulatubbee, at a trading post on the Natchez Road called Doak's Stand. The treaty's own opening lines are worth reading carefully, since they describe their purpose in the government's own words: to promote "the civilization of the Choctaw Indians, by the establishment of schools amongst them," and, in the treaty's own phrase, "to perpetuate them as a nation" by exchanging, for only "a small part of their land," a country beyond the Mississippi River. Mississippi, the treaty notes separately, wanted "a small part of the land belonging to said nation" as well.

Read closely, this is a genuinely limited document. It ceded a specific tract of Choctaw land in central Mississippi, described in Article I by landmarks like the Natchez Road and Black Creek, in exchange for a much larger tract west of the Mississippi River between the Arkansas and Red Rivers, granted to the Choctaw Nation under Article II. It funded Choctaw schools with proceeds from selling some of the ceded land. It offered supplies, a blacksmith, and an agent to those who chose to move west, using language throughout that describes voluntary relocation, aiding "the poor Indians, who wish to remove," rather than a command that everyone must go.

The treaty also reveals something about how these negotiations actually worked in practice, beyond the grand language of its preamble. Article X names specific individuals and specific dollar amounts, five hundred dollars to Mingo Puckshenubbe, two hundred dollars each to several named chiefs and warriors, as compensation for valuable buildings they would lose if they chose to relocate. Article IX guaranteed that any Choctaw family with a separate settlement inside the ceded land could keep a full square mile around their home if they preferred to stay rather than move west at all. These are not the details of a single, sweeping decision handed down from above. They are the details of a negotiated document, worked out person by person, family by family, between people who had real choices in front of them at that particular table in 1820.

Nothing in the Treaty of Doak's Stand itself required the Choctaw Nation to give up the rest of its Mississippi homeland or to relocate as a whole people. That would come later, and not automatically. Whether an 1820 treaty limited to "a small part" of the land was ever going to be followed by a much larger one a decade later depended on choices and pressures that had not yet fully appeared in 1820, a point this lesson returns to directly in Part 2.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT CHANGED IN TEN YEARS

From "a Small Part" to Everything

By 1830, the political situation the Choctaw Nation faced had changed in specific, identifiable ways. Mississippi's legislature passed laws extending state jurisdiction directly over Choctaw persons and property within land the state claimed, effectively announcing that Choctaw citizens living under their own nation's laws would now also be subject to Mississippi's. The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, negotiated that September, states this directly in its own preamble: "the General Assembly of the State Mississippi has extended the laws of said state to persons and property within the charged limits of the same, and the President of the United States has said that they cannot protect the Choctaw people from the operation of those laws."

That sentence is worth sitting with. It is the federal government stating plainly, in the treaty's own words, that it would not or could not shield the Choctaw Nation from a state's assertion of authority over them, even though earlier treaties like Doak's Stand had promised protection and had explicitly aimed to "perpetuate" the Choctaw as a nation on their existing land. Judged by the standard this course applies throughout, whether a government successfully solves the first-order problem of protecting those it has entered into formal relations with, this is the federal government admitting, in writing, a failure at exactly that task.

This shift was not inevitable in 1820. It depended on Mississippi's legislature specifically choosing to extend its laws over the Choctaw Nation, on a change in national political leadership that made removal federal policy rather than one option among several, and on the federal government specifically choosing not to contest a state's claim of authority over a nation it had treated with directly. Andrew Jackson, the same commissioner who had helped negotiate Doak's Stand's more limited terms in 1820, was president of the United States by 1830, a genuinely different man in a genuinely different office, facing a genuinely different set of pressures than he had a decade earlier.

This state-level pressure was not unique to Mississippi. Georgia was pursuing an almost identical strategy against the Cherokee Nation at the same time, extending its own state law over Cherokee land and people. Congress added to this pressure directly by passing the Indian Removal Act in May 1830, months before the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek was even negotiated, making removal official federal policy rather than one option a president might or might not pursue. Taken together, state governments asserting authority over Native nations and a president willing to make removal the federal government's stated goal reinforced each other, each making the other easier to carry out, in a way that neither alone might have accomplished as completely or as quickly.

None of this means removal was destined from the moment Europeans first arrived in Mississippi, or that the 1820 treaty was secretly always going to lead here. It means specific, datable choices, by Mississippi's legislature, by the federal government, and by individual leaders occupying different offices at different times, turned a limited 1820 exchange into something total by 1830.
