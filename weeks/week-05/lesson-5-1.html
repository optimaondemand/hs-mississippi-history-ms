📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
France, Britain, and Spain each wanted the same Native nations as allies, and each nation had to decide which European power, if any, actually served its own interests.
Mississippi's Native nations were not passive bystanders as European powers arrived. This lesson looks at how the Choctaw, Chickasaw, and smaller nations like the Biloxi built trade relationships and alliances with European powers, and how European rivalries sometimes fueled conflict between Native nations themselves, with consequences that could be as severe as the loss of an entire nation's independence.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
alliance (uh-LY-uhns): a formal agreement between groups to cooperate, often for mutual defense or trade.
Biloxi (buh-LUHK-see): a small Native American nation whose homeland centered on Mississippi's Gulf Coast.
cession (SESH-uhn): the formal giving up of land or rights, usually through a treaty.
confederacy (kuhn-FED-er-uh-see): a group of nations or towns joined together for a common purpose while keeping some independence.
📚 PART 1 · TRADE AND ALLIANCE

Choosing Partners Carefully

When French, British, and Spanish traders began arriving in the region in significant numbers, Mississippi's Native nations did not simply accept whichever power showed up first. Trade offered real advantages, European goods like firearms, metal tools, and cloth that were difficult or impossible to produce locally, and Native nations actively negotiated for the best terms, sometimes playing rival European powers against one another to get them.

The deerskin trade sat at the center of this exchange. Native hunters supplied enormous quantities of processed deer hides, valuable in Europe for making leather goods, in return for firearms, ammunition, cloth, metal tools, and other manufactured items. This trade reshaped hunting practices across the region, as demand for deerskins to trade sometimes exceeded what nations had hunted purely for their own use in earlier generations. It also created real economic dependence in both directions. European colonies relied on the deerskin trade for a major source of income, while Native nations increasingly relied on European manufactured goods for tools and weapons they had once made entirely themselves.

The Choctaw generally leaned toward alliance with France, whose colony of Louisiana bordered much of Choctaw territory and whose traders offered goods the Choctaw wanted. The Chickasaw, by contrast, built a closer trading relationship with British colonists based in Carolina, who could offer competitive prices and steady supplies moving overland along established trading paths. Neither alliance was permanent or absolute. Both nations continued to weigh their own interests against whatever a given European power was offering at a given moment, and both maintained some level of contact with multiple European powers even while leaning toward one in particular, keeping their options open in a world where any single ally's fortunes could change quickly.

Smaller nations faced this same calculation on an even more urgent scale. The Biloxi, whose homeland centered on Mississippi's Gulf Coast, were among the first Native peoples to make sustained contact with French colonists arriving at the Gulf in the late 1690s. With a smaller population than the Choctaw or Chickasaw, the Biloxi had less room for error in choosing how to respond to European arrival, and their name would eventually be given to the French settlement, and later the modern city, built near their homeland, a small but lasting mark left on the map by one of the region's smaller nations.

Spain, though less directly involved in these early trading networks than France or Britain, remained a presence to the west and south, controlling territory in Texas and Florida that bordered the region and periodically competing for influence with Native nations near those borders. After 1763, when Britain gained control of West Florida following a major European war, Spain's role in the region grew more direct still, setting up a three-way competition for Native alliance that you'll see unfold further when this course reaches the colonial and Revolutionary periods.

📚 PART 2 · WHEN RIVALRY TURNED TO CONFLICT

Proxy Conflict in the 1700s

The Choctaw-French and Chickasaw-British alliances did not stay peacefully separate. Through much of the 1700s, periods of armed conflict broke out between the Choctaw and Chickasaw, conflicts that were shaped, and at times actively encouraged, by French and British colonial officials eager to weaken a rival power's Native ally. France supplied the Choctaw with weapons partly to pressure the Chickasaw, whose trade relationship with British Carolina threatened French colonial interests along the Mississippi River. This did not mean the Choctaw and Chickasaw had no reasons of their own to compete. It means their own conflicts became entangled with, and in some periods intensified by, a larger European rivalry that had little to do with the original causes of tension between the two nations.

The Natchez experienced this same dynamic with even more severe consequences. Rising tensions with French colonists over land near the Natchez's own principal town erupted into open conflict in 1729. The French, with support from Choctaw allies, ultimately defeated the Natchez decisively, killing or enslaving much of the population and permanently scattering the survivors, some of whom found refuge among the Chickasaw and Creek. The Natchez as a politically independent nation with its own territory effectively ceased to exist after this conflict, a stark illustration of how quickly an alliance built on trade advantage could turn into a fight for a nation's very survival. It is worth returning here to a point made in Week 1: this outcome was not inevitable from the moment French colonists first settled near Natchez territory. Specific decisions, disputes over specific land, specific timing, pushed a tense relationship into open war. A different sequence of choices on either side might well have produced a different outcome entirely.

This pattern, Native nations pursuing their own interests within relationships that also served European colonial competition, appears throughout Mississippi's colonial period. It would matter enormously again during the American Revolution, when Spain, France, Britain, and the newly independent United States all sought alliances or neutrality from the same Native nations, each for reasons that had little to do with what any Native nation actually wanted for itself. The Choctaw and Chickasaw, having already survived decades of being courted, armed, and occasionally used against one another by rival colonial powers, entered this next round of competition with hard-won experience in weighing which promises from which power were actually worth trusting.
