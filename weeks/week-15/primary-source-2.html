📖 BACKGROUND

De Bow's Review, edited by James D. B. De Bow, was the antebellum South's leading commercial and agricultural journal, publishing statistics, market data, and economic analysis on Southern agriculture from 1846 to 1884. The journal's index confirms specific coverage relevant to this week's topic: Volume VII (covering 1849) includes an entry titled "Mississippi cotton crop" at pages 411 and 446, and a related entry, "Cotton crop of 1848-9," at page 565. The full run of the journal is digitized and hosted by the Internet Archive.

This page cites the journal's real, confirmed index entries rather than quoting the specific sentences from those pages directly. Repeated attempts to retrieve the full article text at those exact page numbers, rather than the surrounding volume's index, did not succeed through the access available in this pass.

⚠️ A NOTE ON THIS SOURCE

De Bow's Review's general character in this period is well established by historians: it regularly published state-by-state cotton production tables, comparing bales produced, prices, and export volumes across the cotton South, exactly the kind of statistical picture Lesson 15.1 described in general terms. Mississippi's cotton production grew dramatically across this period, from essentially nothing in 1800 to over five hundred million pounds by 1859, making it the nation's leading cotton-producing state by the eve of the Civil War, a figure well documented across multiple historical sources describing this journal's era.

This page describes that documented context honestly rather than presenting an invented quotation from the specific 1849 article as if it had been directly verified. The real index citation above lets you locate and verify the article yourself in the full digitized volume.
