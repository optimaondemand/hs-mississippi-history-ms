📚 PART 1 · WHO ACTUALLY OWNED MISSISSIPPI'S WEALTH

A Steep Pyramid, Not a Broad Middle Class

Antebellum Mississippi's white population was itself sharply divided by wealth, in ways the term "planter" often obscures. A genuine planter, by the common historical definition, owned twenty or more enslaved people; by that measure, planters made up only a small minority of Mississippi's white households, even though they controlled a hugely disproportionate share of the state's best land and enslaved labor. Most white Mississippians were yeoman farmers who owned no enslaved people at all, or a handful at most, working smaller farms on less desirable land, often in the hill country away from the richest cotton soil.

This week's Primary Source 15.1 is a real planter's own diary, kept by Everard Green Baker, who owned plantations in Jefferson, Panola, and Hinds Counties between 1848 and 1876. Diaries and ledgers like Baker's typically recorded exactly the kind of detail that made a plantation function as a business: crop yields, weather, purchases and debts, correspondence with the cotton factors and merchants who financed and sold a planter's crop, and direct records of the enslaved people whose labor the entire operation depended on entirely.

A document like this is valuable precisely because it shows the ordinary, day-to-day mechanics behind the large historical forces Lesson 15.1 described. Behind the abstract statistics about cotton exports and land prices sat actual people making actual decisions, an owner's daily entries about weather and yields, sitting on the same page as entries about the enslaved people forced to produce them, a juxtaposition that is itself part of what this kind of source reveals honestly.

Larger plantations often employed a hired overseer to manage the daily details of enslaved labor directly, freeing the owner for the financial and social side of running an estate, correspondence with cotton factors in Natchez or New Orleans who advanced credit against an unharvested crop and then sold that crop once picked, keeping a share of the proceeds for themselves. This credit system tied planters, banks, and factors together in a single financial chain, one where a bad cotton price in a given year could ripple through debts owed at every link, from the largest planter down to the local merchants who depended on planter business to survive.

Labor on a cotton plantation was typically organized under what historians call the gang system, enslaved workers grouped together and moved through a field in a coordinated line, each person expected to keep pace with the group under an overseer's direct supervision, a method designed to maximize the amount of cotton picked each day rather than to accommodate any individual worker's own pace or wellbeing. Ledgers like Baker's frequently recorded yields per hand, a phrase itself worth pausing on, since it reduces a human being's labor to a unit of agricultural output, exactly the kind of language a plantation's own records normalized without comment.

📚 PART 2 · DID THIS ECONOMY SOLVE ITS FIRST-ORDER PROBLEM?

Prosperity for Whom?

This course has repeatedly asked whether a given economic or political system solves the first-order problem of producing and distributing enough for general prosperity across the whole population it governs, not merely for its most powerful members. Mississippi's cotton economy, judged honestly by that standard, is not a close call. It generated enormous wealth, but that wealth flowed overwhelmingly to a small planter class, while the enslaved majority who actually grew and picked the cotton received none of it, held no property of their own by law, and had no legal standing to claim any share of what their forced labor produced.

Even judged only by the free, white population's own prosperity, the picture is uneven rather than uniformly successful. Yeoman farmers without significant enslaved labor of their own often struggled to compete with large plantations for the best land and access to credit, and many found their own economic mobility narrowing precisely as planter wealth expanded around them. A cotton economy this concentrated in a small class's hands is a genuinely limited answer to the first-order problem of general prosperity, even before accounting for the enslaved population it depended on and excluded from that prosperity entirely.

A defender of this system at the time might have pointed to its real, undeniable productivity, Mississippi genuinely did produce enormous quantities of cotton feeding textile mills as far away as Britain, and argued that productivity itself was evidence of a well-functioning economy. That argument mistakes total output for general prosperity, exactly the distinction this course's Classical Republican standard insists on holding apart. A system can be extraordinarily productive in the aggregate while still failing the great majority of the people whose labor makes that productivity possible, and judging an economy only by its total wealth, without asking who actually held that wealth, is precisely the kind of incomplete evaluation this course asks you to avoid.

None of this erases the real industry, planning, and enterprise this week's virtue names, real work went into building this economy, by planters, by yeoman farmers, and, most of all, by the enslaved people whose forced labor made it possible. But industry alone does not make an economic system just, and a system's productivity is not the same question as whether it distributes what it produces fairly. Holding both truths, real economic achievement and a deeply unjust distribution of its rewards, is exactly the discipline this course asks of you as it turns, starting next week, to the people this economy was built on top of.
