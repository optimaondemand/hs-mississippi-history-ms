## Primary Source Exercise 15.1 — The Everard Green Baker Papers

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** Why might a planter's diary record weather, crop yields, and enslaved people's labor on the same pages, without treating that juxtaposition as remarkable?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why would a large plantation owner keep detailed written records at all, rather than simply managing the operation from memory?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose Baker's diary had been written specifically for publication rather than for his own private use. How might its content and tone have likely differed?
4. **(Conceptual)** This page describes a real, cataloged collection without quoting a specific diary entry. Why is that distinction, between a source's confirmed existence and its confirmed specific content, important for a historian to maintain honestly?

## Primary Source Exercise 15.2 — De Bow's Review on Mississippi Cotton

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): historical causation, conceptual, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Historical causation)** Why would a Southern commercial journal in the 1840s and 1850s dedicate regular coverage to state-by-state cotton production statistics?
2. **(Conceptual)** A journal like De Bow's Review served planters, merchants, and bankers with real financial interests in cotton's continued profitability. What does that intended audience suggest about how its statistics and analysis might be framed?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose cotton prices had collapsed sharply in the late 1840s instead of remaining strong. How might that have changed what a journal like De Bow's Review chose to publish and emphasize?
4. **(Conceptual)** This page cites a real index entry (specific volume and page numbers) without quoting the article's exact sentences. Why does citing a specific, verifiable location still add real value even without a direct quotation?
