📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Eight weeks ago, this quarter began with a hurricane-battered Spanish fleet sailing up the Mississippi. It ends with a state that has built its own government, its own constitution, and, on top of both, a plantation economy no honest accounting can separate from the people it enslaved.
This review lesson does not introduce new material. It draws together the threads from Weeks 10 through 17 so you can see the whole arc of Semester 1's second quarter at once, before the Quarter 2 exam and the comprehensive Semester 1 final.

📚 PART 1 · BUILDING A POLITICAL COMMUNITY

From Conquest to Constitution

This quarter's first five weeks trace Mississippi's path from a contested colonial territory to a fully organized state, a path this course's self-government virtue thread followed closely. Week 10 opened with Bernardo de Galvez's Gulf Coast campaign, a hurricane-battered gamble that removed British power from the lower Mississippi in three weeks, followed immediately by the 1781 Natchez uprising, a reminder that a treaty settles a question on paper far more cleanly than in the minds of the people actually living through it. Week 11 turned to the Choctaw Nation's own removal, tracing a genuine ten-year gap between the limited Treaty of Doak's Stand (1820) and the total Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (1830), a gap explained by specific, datable choices, not inevitable destiny.

Weeks 12 through 14 then built the state itself. The 1798 Territory Act organized the land, deliberately excluding the Northwest Ordinance's ban on slavery from the start. The 1817 Enabling Act split that territory in two, admitting only its western half as Mississippi. The state's first government under David Holmes, its move to a purpose-built capital named for Galvez's own negotiating partner in the Doak's Stand treaty, and its competitive Jacksonian-era party politics all followed. Week 14's 1817 constitution capped this arc: genuine self-government, grounded explicitly in popular sovereignty, standing alongside a constitutional article protecting slavery every bit as deliberately as the rest of the document protected liberty.

📚 PART 2 · AN ECONOMY AND A SOCIETY BUILT ON BONDAGE

Cotton, Slavery, and Antebellum Society

1793
Eli Whitney's cotton gin makes short-staple cotton dramatically more profitable, setting up the economic boom Weeks 11 and 12's newly opened land would soon fuel.
1817
Mississippi's constitution protects slavery explicitly in Article VI, the same year it establishes genuine self-government elsewhere in the same document.
1821–1822
The capital moves to a newly founded city named Jackson, and the Flush Times land boom accelerates across the newly ceded Delta and prairie regions.
1830s–1840s
Mississippi's enslaved population becomes a majority of the state's total population, driven substantially by the forced domestic slave trade.
1857
The Revised Code compiles Mississippi's detailed slave code, governing enslaved people's movement, assembly, and punishment.
1860
Frederick Law Olmsted publishes his account of Mississippi's three-tiered antebellum society; Mississippi stands among the nation's wealthiest states on the eve of the Civil War.

Weeks 15 through 17 turned from political structure to economic and social reality. Week 15 traced the cotton gin, newly ceded land, and enslaved labor combining to produce Mississippi's antebellum wealth, wealth concentrated overwhelmingly in a small planter class rather than distributed broadly. Week 16 examined that labor system directly: the domestic slave trade that drove much of Mississippi's population growth, the Forks of the Road market at Natchez, the 1857 code's detailed legal control over enslaved people's daily lives, and real testimony from Mississippians who lived under that system, recorded decades later by WPA interviewers and read with care for the conditions under which it was given.

Week 17 completed the picture with a full social portrait, planters, poor whites, and the enslaved, seen through an outside traveler's critical eye and through a runaway advertisement revealing one enslaved man's own resistance to the system built around him. Taken together, Weeks 10 through 17 tell one connected story: a political community that solved real first-order problems of government and self-rule, while building its prosperity on a system that failed the basic test of justice for the majority of people living under it. That tension, not a simple story of progress or a simple story of failure, is the honest shape of this quarter, and it is exactly the tension the Civil War, which Quarter 3 takes up directly, would eventually force the whole nation to confront.
