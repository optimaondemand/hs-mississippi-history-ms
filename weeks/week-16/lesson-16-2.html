💡 GLOSSARY
These words carried over from Lesson 16.1 are still worth reviewing.
chattel slavery (CHAT-uhl SLAY-vuh-ree): a system of slavery in which enslaved people were legally classified as property that could be bought, sold, and inherited, rather than as persons with legal rights.
enslaved (en-SLAYVD): held in bondage as legal property, denied freedom and basic rights by force of law; preferred over "slave" alone because it names something done to a person, not what that person fundamentally is.
📚 PART 1 · A RACE AGAINST TIME

Collecting Testimony Before It Was Too Late

In the late 1930s, the federal Works Progress Administration, a Depression-era government program employing out-of-work writers among many others, sent interviewers across the South to record the memories of formerly enslaved Americans while any of them were still alive to share them. In Mississippi, interviewers spoke with roughly four hundred fifty formerly enslaved people, most of them by then in their seventies, eighties, or older, recalling events from six or more decades earlier. The Library of Congress preserves these interviews today as part of a collection titled Born in Slavery.

These narratives capture a genuinely wide range of memory and perspective, which is itself an important lesson about avoiding any single, flattened account of what slavery meant to everyone who lived through it. James Lucas of Adams County, reflecting on the sharecropping system that followed emancipation, said it plainly: it "wuzn't much diffent from slavery." Charlie Moses of Lincoln County recalled that "Slavery Days wuz bitter, bitter, an' I shall never fo'git the sufferin." Virginia Harris of Coahoma County put the core reality starkly: "When you is a slave, you ain't got no mo' chance than a bullfrog."

Not every narrative sounds the same, and that range is real, not a contradiction to smooth over. Some accounts carry a real defiance. Belle Caruthers of Marshall County offered a version of a phrase with deep roots in America's own founding language: "Give me freedom, or give me death." A small number of other narrators, interviewed in the specific, pressured circumstances Part 2 examines directly, offered more favorable descriptions of their own individual owners, such as Adam Smith of Tate County, who recalled that "our white folks… were good to us." Other testimony is unflinching about violence and loss, as when Henry Walton of Marshall County recalled that his mother was whipped to death when he was only three or four years old, a memory carried for the rest of his life and finally recorded some seven decades later.

Reading across this full range of testimony, rather than selecting only the accounts that fit one predetermined conclusion, is exactly the kind of honest engagement with primary sources this course asks of you throughout. One further note on how these narratives were recorded: WPA interviewers often transcribed narrators' speech using an exaggerated, phonetic spelling meant to represent regional dialect, a convention historians today treat with real caution, since it risked making educated, articulate speakers sound less serious on the page than their actual words and reasoning warranted. The content of what a narrator said matters more than the spelling an interviewer chose to render it in.

📚 PART 2 · READING THESE SOURCES CAREFULLY

What Made Some Narrators Guarded

Historians treat these narratives as genuinely valuable evidence, but not as simple, unfiltered transcripts free of any distorting pressure. Most interviews were conducted by white interviewers, in the South of the 1930s, during a period when Black Mississippians faced serious, ongoing legal and social pressure to describe race relations, past and present, cautiously. Two narrators' own words make this pressure explicit rather than leaving it for a historian to infer. Liza McGhee of Marshall County told her interviewer plainly, "I don't want to say nothing that will get me in bondage again." Rhoda Hunt, born in 1854, put it just as directly: "My conscience tells me to keep my mouth shet."

These two statements are themselves genuine historical evidence, not a flaw that undermines the narratives around them. They tell you directly that the conditions of the interview itself, an elderly Black Mississippian speaking to a government interviewer decades after slavery within a still-segregated society, shaped what could safely be said and how. A narrative describing a kind owner in favorable terms may reflect an honest memory, a genuine survival strategy learned under slavery and never fully safe to abandon afterward, or some combination of both, and a careful historian reads each account with that context in view rather than accepting every word at face value or dismissing every account as unreliable.

Read together with Mississippi's 1857 slave code from Lesson 16.1, these narratives complete a picture the legal document alone could never provide. The code tells you what the law required and permitted. The narratives tell you what living under that law actually felt like, in the words of the people who had no choice but to live it, weighed carefully against the real conditions under which those words were eventually spoken. This week's virtue is justice, and an honest reckoning with both kinds of evidence together, the system's own rules and the testimony of those it was built to control, is exactly what that virtue demands of you here.

It is worth naming plainly, at the close of this lesson, what these two weeks together have actually shown: a legal and economic system that dispossessed people of their freedom, their families, and any legal claim to the wealth their own labor produced, defended in a state constitution's own careful language, enforced by patrols and courts, and remembered, decades later, by people who had lived every part of it and still, in some cases, weighed their words with real caution before an interviewer they had every reason not to fully trust. That is the honest weight this material carries, and this course asks you to sit with it rather than move past it too quickly.
