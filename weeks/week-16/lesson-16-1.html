📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
By 1860, Mississippi held one of the highest shares of enslaved people of any state in the country, a population that had grown not by natural increase alone but through the forced transport of tens of thousands of people from other states.
Weeks 14 and 15 have already shown you that slavery was legally protected in Mississippi's constitution and economically central to its cotton economy. This lesson looks directly at how slavery itself grew and changed in Mississippi between statehood and the Civil War, both in raw numbers and in the specific legal system that governed enslaved people's daily lives.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
chattel slavery (CHAT-uhl SLAY-vuh-ree): a system of slavery in which enslaved people were legally classified as property that could be bought, sold, and inherited, rather than as persons with legal rights.
slave code (SLAYV kohd): a body of laws specifically regulating enslaved people's behavior, movement, and punishment.
enslaved (en-SLAYVD): held in bondage as legal property, denied freedom and basic rights by force of law; preferred over "slave" alone because it names something done to a person, not what that person fundamentally is.
📚 PART 1 · A POPULATION GROWN BY FORCE

The Domestic Slave Trade

Mississippi's enslaved population did not grow only through children born to families already living in the state. As the cotton economy Week 15 described expanded rapidly, planters' demand for labor outpaced what natural population growth alone could supply, and a large-scale, organized domestic slave trade filled that gap by forcibly transporting enslaved people from Virginia, Maryland, and other Upper South states to Mississippi and neighboring Deep South states. Historians sometimes call this forced migration the Second Middle Passage, a name deliberately echoing the transatlantic slave trade to underscore how brutal and disruptive this internal trade was to the people who endured it, even though it took place entirely within the United States and after the international slave trade's legal end in 1808.

Natchez became one of the largest slave-trading markets in the entire South during this period, with a site known as the Forks of the Road operating as a major center of sale. Tens of thousands of people passed through markets like this one, sold away from parents, spouses, and children with no legal recourse and often no ability to remain in contact afterward. This is not a peripheral detail of Mississippi's antebellum economy. It is a direct, necessary mechanism by which that economy's labor supply actually grew, and it inflicted a specific, repeated form of family separation on enslaved Mississippians that this course's later units on Reconstruction will show enslaved families spent years afterward trying to repair.

By 1860, enslaved people made up more than half of Mississippi's entire population, one of the highest shares of any state in the country. That number is the direct, measurable result of decades of forced migration layered on top of natural population growth, driven by the same cotton economy Week 15 described as extraordinarily profitable for a small planter class. The profit and the population growth are not separate stories. They are the same story, told from two different angles.

Despite the system's deliberate cruelty, enslaved Mississippians built and maintained real family bonds, religious practice, and community wherever the system's own logic left any room for it, and resisted their bondage in ways ranging from quiet daily acts of self-preservation to open flight. None of this softened what the system actually was or asked of the people living under it, but an honest account of slavery in Mississippi has to include the fact that enslaved people were never merely passive objects acted upon. They were people, exercising whatever agency a brutally restrictive system permitted them, a distinction this course's Historiography insists on holding onto rather than flattening into a single story of pure victimhood.

📚 PART 2 · A LEGAL SYSTEM OF CONTROL

What Mississippi's 1857 Slave Code Actually Required

Mississippi's 1857 Revised Code laid out, in specific numbered articles, exactly how enslaved people's movement and behavior were to be controlled. One article required that "no slave shall go from the tenement of his master or employer… without a pass, or some letter or token whereby it may appear that he is proceeding by proper authority," meaning an enslaved person's ability to travel even a short distance depended entirely on a piece of paper someone else controlled. Another article declared any gathering of more than five enslaved people at a public place an "unlawful assembly," directly restricting enslaved people's ability to gather, worship, or organize among themselves without a white person's active oversight.

The code's punishment provisions were severe by design. Assault against a white person by an enslaved person could be punished by up to one hundred lashes, and the code treated certain offenses, including murder, as capital crimes carrying a death sentence. These were not abstract legal categories. They described real physical violence the law explicitly authorized against enslaved people, backed by the full machinery of the state's courts, sheriffs, and organized slave patrols that rode through the countryside checking for enslaved people traveling without a pass.

These patrols were themselves a distinct local institution, typically made up of small groups of white men, both slaveholders and non-slaveholders, assigned to ride or walk an assigned patrol district within their county on a rotating basis. A patrol's authority was considerable and largely unsupervised in the moment: catching an enslaved person without a valid pass could result in immediate, on-the-spot punishment rather than a hearing before any court first. This structure meant that enforcement of the code did not depend only on formal courts and judges. It reached directly into the countryside through ordinary white citizens deputized, in effect, to police enslaved people's movement on any given night.

Recall Week 14's Article VI, which protected slavery constitutionally and barred the legislature from freeing enslaved people without owner consent. This code is what that constitutional protection actually looked like in daily practice: a detailed legal apparatus reaching into where an enslaved person could walk, whom they could gather with, and what punishment awaited any violation, all enforced by courts an enslaved person had no standing to appeal to as an equal party. Judged by this course's Classical Republican standard, a legal system this thoroughly organized around controlling one group for the material benefit of another fails the basic test of justice this week's virtue names directly, regardless of how efficiently or predictably its own internal rules were enforced.
