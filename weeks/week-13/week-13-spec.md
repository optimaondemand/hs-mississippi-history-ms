   a) Natchez was destroyed by a flood

## Primary Source Exercise 13.1 — The Statesman and Gazette, Natchez

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** This page describes a real, cataloged newspaper without quoting a specific issue directly. What is the difference between a source being confirmed real and a source being directly quotable, and why does that distinction matter to a historian?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why might a growing state capital city like Natchez have supported multiple competing newspapers rather than just one?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose only one newspaper had existed in Natchez during the 1828 and 1829 elections. How might political competition in Mississippi have looked different without a rival paper offering an alternative perspective?
4. **(Conceptual)** Nineteenth-century newspaper editors were often open partisans rather than neutral reporters. What does that openness about bias suggest about how readers of this period likely approached what they read, compared to how readers might approach an unfamiliar news source today?

---

## Primary Source Exercise 13.2 — The Southern Galaxy, Natchez

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): historical causation, conceptual, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Historical causation)** Why would a newspaper publisher choose to launch a brand-new paper in 1828, in a city that already had at least one established competing paper?
2. **(Conceptual)** Two competing partisan newspapers in the same small city is itself a form of evidence about that city's political culture, even without a specific quoted article. What does that evidence suggest about Natchez specifically?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose the Southern Galaxy and the Statesman and Gazette had been owned by the same publisher rather than competing independently. How might that have changed the range of political perspectives available to Natchez readers?
4. **(Conceptual)** Why is it more honest for a historian to say "this source's specific content could not be verified" than to guess at what it probably said and present that guess as established fact?

## Discussion

**Prompt:** This week's lessons argued that Mississippi's early party competition was a genuinely mixed case: real, functioning political rivalry, but limited to a narrow slice of the population entitled to vote or hold office. Do you think a political system can be judged a success at self-government if it works well only for the people allowed to participate in it? Use specific details from this week's lessons and primary sources to support your position. Respond to a classmate who reached a different conclusion than you did.
