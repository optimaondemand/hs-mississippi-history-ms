📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On October 12, 1492, Columbus's lookout spotted land. Columbus believed he had reached Asia. He had not, and that mistake reshaped the world.
Columbus's first voyage did not go the way he expected, and what followed his arrival in the Americas was larger and stranger than anyone on either side of the Atlantic could have predicted. This lesson looks at what actually happened when Columbus made contact, why the disease that followed was a consequence of that contact rather than a deliberate plan, and why historians still argue about how to weigh what his voyage set in motion.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

A Voyage That Found the Wrong Thing

Columbus's small fleet left Spain on August 3, 1492, and sailed west for more than two months without sighting land. On October 12, a lookout on the Pinta finally spotted an island in what is now the Bahamas. Columbus was certain he had reached islands near Asia, and he never fully gave up that belief, even after several more voyages. He called the people he met "Indians" because of that mistaken assumption, a label that stuck for centuries afterward even though it described the wrong part of the world entirely.

The Taino approached Columbus's ships in canoes, and the earliest exchanges were largely peaceful. Columbus later wrote that they were generous, willingly trading valuable items for glass beads, small bells, and other trinkets that cost the Spanish almost nothing. He also noted that the Taino had no iron tools and could be easily overpowered by a small, well-armed group, an observation that would shape Spanish policy toward Native peoples in the region for years afterward.

Columbus made contact with the Taino people of the Caribbean, exchanged goods with them, and left a small group of men behind at a settlement called La Navidad before sailing back to Spain to report what he had found. When he arrived home, Ferdinand and Isabella were convinced enough to fund a much larger second voyage. None of this had been planned in advance by anyone, on either side of the ocean. No European in 1492 knew that two entire continents, unknown to them, stood between Europe and Asia. Columbus himself died years later still believing he had reached the edge of Asia.

The difference in scale between Columbus's first and second voyages says a great deal about how fast expectations changed. His first fleet had carried roughly ninety men on three small ships. His second, launched in 1493 within a year of his return, carried around seventeen ships and over a thousand men, including soldiers, priests, and colonists intending to settle permanently rather than simply explore and report back. Spain was no longer testing an idea. It was moving to claim territory. Portugal noticed the shift too, which is part of why the two crowns moved so quickly to settle their competing claims through the treaty you'll read next.

📚 PART 2 · A WORLD STARTS TO CHANGE

The Columbian Exchange

Historians call the flow of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492 the Columbian Exchange. Crops that had grown only in the Americas, such as maize, potatoes, and tomatoes, eventually reshaped diets and fueled population growth across Europe, Africa, and Asia. Animals unknown in the Americas before contact, including horses, cattle, and pigs, arrived on European ships and transformed how Native peoples farmed, traveled, and made war.

The exchange ran in both directions, but its effects were uneven, and disease moved almost entirely one way: from the Eastern Hemisphere into the Americas, with the consequences described in the next section. None of this exchange was coordinated or planned by any single government. It emerged gradually out of thousands of individual voyages, much like Columbus's own, each adding another thread to a pattern nobody at the time could see in full. Crops like sugar cane, originally from Asia, would eventually reshape entire economies across the Americas, including the plantation economy this course returns to later in Mississippi's own history, generations after Columbus's ships first crossed the Atlantic.

📚 PART 3 · CONTACT, DISEASE, AND CONSEQUENCE

A Catastrophe Nobody Planned

Estimates vary and remain debated among historians, but many believe that within a century or two of sustained contact, more than ninety percent of the pre-contact population of the Americas had died, an almost unimaginable loss. The overwhelming cause was disease: smallpox, measles, influenza, and other illnesses that had circulated in Europe, Asia, and Africa for centuries but had never reached the Americas before. Native peoples had no prior exposure and no built-up immunity, and these diseases spread from community to community, often racing ahead of the Europeans themselves.

This matters for how we explain what happened, not just what happened. Nobody in 1492 understood how disease spread, and no European government planned a biological attack on Native peoples. The population collapse was a consequence of contact between two populations that had been separated for thousands of years, not a deliberate campaign. Treating it as though it were planned in advance gets the history wrong in an important way: it assumes people intended an outcome they had no way of understanding, let alone engineering. That does not make the outcome any less catastrophic for the people who lived through it. It changes what kind of historical event we are actually explaining.

This is part of why historians still argue about how to judge Columbus. His voyage opened permanent, sustained contact between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres for the first time in human history, a genuine turning point that reshaped trade, food, disease, and power across the entire globe. It also set in motion, unintentionally, one of the largest population losses in human history. Both of those things are true at the same time, and reasonable historians weigh them differently. The disagreement is less about the facts than about how much weight each side of that record deserves.

This disagreement has not stayed confined to history classrooms. Some cities and states now mark the second Monday of October as Indigenous Peoples' Day instead of, or alongside, Columbus Day, reflecting the view that the day should center the experience of Native peoples rather than celebrate the explorer. Others continue to observe Columbus Day as it has traditionally been marked. Recent debates over the holiday are, in part, the same historians' disagreement described above, playing out in how a society chooses to remember its own past.
