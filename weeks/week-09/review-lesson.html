📚 PART 1 · THE LAND AND ITS FIRST PEOPLES

Geography Comes First

This quarter began with geography for a reason: everything that followed depended on it. Weeks 2 and 3 established Mississippi's major regions, the Delta, the Hills, the Piney Woods, and the Gulf Coast, and showed how the Mississippi River and Gulf Coast built two genuinely different economies, one centered on river commerce and cotton, the other on shipping and trade with other Gulf ports. That same geography also produced disaster: the levee failures of 1927 and the hurricanes that have repeatedly struck the Coast, Camille in 1969 and Katrina in 2005, showed that the same features that built Mississippi's economy could also destroy what people built on top of it.

Weeks 4 and 5 turned to the people who lived on this land first. The Mississippian mound-building culture, sustained by the same fertile river soil, produced complex chiefdoms and sites like Winterville Mounds. The Natchez carried this Mississippian pattern into the historical period, giving Europeans a rare direct look at a living version of it, while the Choctaw and Chickasaw built different, more distributed forms of government spread across many towns. All three nations, along with smaller nations like the Biloxi, built trade relationships and alliances with European powers, alliances that sometimes turned Native rivalries into proxy conflicts, and that in the Natchez's case ended in the loss of political independence entirely by 1731. Yet Native history in Mississippi did not end there, as the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians' continued self-government today makes clear.

📚 PART 2 · THREE FLAGS OVER THE SAME LAND

Spain, France, and England, in Sequence

1540–1541
De Soto's Spanish expedition passes through Mississippi, fights the Chickasaw at Chicaza, crosses the Mississippi River, and leaves without founding any lasting settlement.
1699
Iberville's French expedition builds Fort Maurepas near Biloxi, the first lasting European settlement, more than a century after De Soto and seventeen years after La Salle's ceremonial claim.
1724
France issues the Code Noir for Louisiana, regulating enslaved labor and religious life, a code whose influence outlasts French rule itself.
1763
The Treaty of Paris transfers the Natchez District from France to Britain, forming part of the new colony of British West Florida.
1779
Spain seizes West Florida from Britain during the American Revolution, beginning a new Spanish period in the Natchez District.

Weeks 6 through 8 traced this sequence of European powers directly. Each power arrived with different priorities, Spanish conquest, French trade and labor, English land-grant settlement, and each left something behind that outlasted its own political control: Spain's early contact and its disease legacy, France's Code Noir and place names, England's land-grant patterns and English-speaking settlers. None of these outcomes were inevitable. Contingency, not destiny, decided which power controlled which piece of land at which moment, decisions made as often in European treaty rooms as on Mississippi soil itself.

Notice, too, how many of this quarter's themes echo across weeks that otherwise seem unrelated. The gap between a formal claim and actual control, first raised with La Salle's 1682 ceremony, applies just as well to European claims over Native land generally. The idea that institutions outlast the governments that create them, first raised with the Code Noir's influence on later slave codes, applies just as well to the land claims Spain preserved from British rule in the Natchez District. A quarter that might look, week by week, like a series of separate topics is really one connected argument about geography, contact, and the layered, contingent way Mississippi's history actually unfolded.
