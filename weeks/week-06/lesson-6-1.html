📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A Spanish army of six hundred men marched into what is now Mississippi more than eighty years before the first English colonists reached Jamestown.
Hernando de Soto's expedition was the first sustained European presence on the land that would become Mississippi. This lesson traces how his army got there, what it was looking for, and what its arrival meant for the Native nations already living on that land, more than a century before any European power attempted permanent settlement in the region.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
conquistador (kon-KEES-tuh-dor): a Spanish or Portuguese soldier and adventurer who took part in conquering land in the Americas during the 1500s and 1600s.
expedition (ek-spuh-DISH-uhn): a journey made by a group of people for a specific purpose, such as exploring unfamiliar land.
📚 PART 1 · A CONQUISTADOR'S AMBITION

Why de Soto Came

Hernando de Soto had already made a fortune helping conquer the Inca Empire in Peru, and he returned to Spain wealthy and ambitious for a command of his own. In 1537, the Spanish crown granted him the right to explore and conquer "La Florida," a vague Spanish term that at the time referred to nearly the entire southeastern portion of North America, far larger than the modern state of Florida, and a territory whose actual size and geography the Spanish crown itself barely understood when it issued the grant. De Soto financed and organized an expedition of roughly six hundred men, along with horses, war dogs, and pigs intended as a mobile food supply, and departed Spain in 1538, stopping first in Cuba, where he served briefly as governor, before launching the mainland expedition itself the following year with the bulk of his men, horses, and supplies intact.

The pigs mattered more than they might seem to at first. Unlike horses or dogs, pigs could forage for much of their own food and reproduce quickly, giving the expedition a self-renewing meat supply that did not depend entirely on what could be taken or traded from Native towns along the way. By the time the expedition reached Mississippi, the herd had grown substantially from its original size, a detail that later became important when the herd itself became a target during a conflict you'll read about in the next lesson, and a reminder that even a well-planned expedition's fortunes could turn on something as ordinary as its food supply.

De Soto's motivations were straightforward by the standards of Spanish conquest at the time: he hoped to find another wealthy empire like the Inca or Aztec, rich in gold and silver, and to conquer it for personal fortune and royal favor. After landing in Florida in 1539, his expedition spent nearly a year moving north and west through what is now Georgia, South Carolina, and Alabama, fighting, negotiating, and demanding food and porters from Native towns along the way, before finally crossing into the region that is now Mississippi in late 1540.

This long overland route mattered. By the time De Soto's expedition reached Mississippi, it had already fought a major, costly battle against a Native confederacy in Alabama, at a fortified town called Mabila, that killed a significant number of Spanish soldiers and destroyed much of the expedition's remaining supplies and equipment. The expedition that entered Mississippi in late 1540, in other words, was not the same well-equipped force that had landed in Florida the year before. It was smaller, more depleted, and more desperate to find the wealth that had justified the entire venture in the first place, a desperation that would shape its behavior toward the Native nations it still had to pass through.

📚 PART 2 · A DIFFERENT KIND OF EUROPEAN ARRIVAL

Exploration, Not Settlement

De Soto's expedition looked very different from the French, English, and later American settlement Mississippi would experience in later centuries, and it is worth being precise about that difference now, since you'll be comparing all three European powers directly in the coming weeks. De Soto's men built no permanent towns, established no lasting government, and left behind no colony. They moved constantly, staying only as long as a location could feed them, then moving on in search of wealth that never materialized.

Contact with Native nations along the route also looked different from later colonial patterns. De Soto's expedition typically arrived at a town, demanded food, shelter, and often porters or guides, and used force or the threat of it when a town's leaders resisted. This was closer to a military occupation passing through than to trade or settlement. Some encounters were peaceful, particularly with towns willing to comply quickly, but many were not, and the expedition left a trail of damaged relationships with Native nations across the entire Southeast, not only in Mississippi.

De Soto's men also relied heavily throughout the journey on captured Native interpreters and guides, some traveling with the expedition for years, often against their will. One interpreter, a Spanish castaway named Juan Ortiz who had been living among a Florida nation for over a decade before De Soto's arrival, proved invaluable to the expedition's ability to communicate at all in its early stages. Without interpreters like Ortiz, the entire expedition's ability to negotiate, or even to understand basic warnings, would have been far more limited than it already was, especially once the expedition moved beyond the range of any language its interpreters already knew.

This made De Soto's presence in Mississippi brief by later colonial standards, likely less than a year in total, but it does not mean the expedition left no impact. His forces demanded and often took food, supplies, and forced labor from Native towns already struggling to support their own populations. Perhaps more consequentially, and unintentionally, the expedition likely introduced European diseases into Native communities that had no prior exposure or immunity to them, setting off the same kind of catastrophic population decline described earlier this quarter, though on a more localized and immediate scale wherever the expedition actually passed.
