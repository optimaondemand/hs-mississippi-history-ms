## Reading quiz (7 questions, covers both lessons, inference-based)

1. **The Mississippi Delta counts as a distinct alluvial region mainly because:**
   a) it sits at the mouth of the river, where it meets the Gulf
   b) it was the first area in the state to be settled by Europeans
   c) it was built up over thousands of years by repeated river flooding *(correct)*
   d) it has the highest elevation of any region in the state

2. **The Black Prairie's distinctive dark, chalky soil formed because:**
   a) volcanic ash settled there millions of years ago
   b) an ancient shallow sea once covered the region *(correct)*
   c) it was originally part of the Mississippi River's channel
   d) settlers imported soil from other states to improve farmland

3. **Before large-scale timber harvesting, the Piney Woods region supported which industry?**
   a) Naval stores production (tar, pitch, and turpentine) *(correct)*
   b) Rice cultivation
   c) Fishing and shipbuilding
   d) Sugar refining

4. **Mississippi's western border follows the Mississippi River rather than a surveyed line because:**
   a) Congress required all Southern states to use rivers as borders
   b) the border was set by geography rather than by a drawn line *(correct)*
   c) the river was the only accurate way to measure distance in the 1800s
   d) Louisiana and Mississippi share identical legal codes

5. **Which of the following is a responsibility handled by Mississippi county government rather than the state government directly?**
   a) Setting statewide tax rates
   b) Appointing the governor
   c) Writing the state constitution
   d) Maintaining local roads and land records *(correct)*

6. **Mississippi's number of counties grew gradually after statehood mainly because:**
   a) new counties were created as settlement expanded and existing counties grew too large to serve conveniently *(correct)*
   b) the state constitution required a new county every five years
   c) neighboring states transferred territory to Mississippi repeatedly
   d) each Mississippi governor was required to create one new county

7. **Compared with Alabama's 67 counties and Louisiana's parish system, Mississippi's 82 counties show that:**
   a) all Southern states eventually adopted identical systems of local government
   b) states with more people always have more counties
   c) there was no single correct way for a state to divide its own territory *(correct)*
   d) county government was a failed experiment abandoned everywhere except Mississippi

## Primary Source Exercise 2.1 — Report on the Geology and Agriculture of Mississippi (1860)

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** Why might a state-commissioned scientific report choose to open by correcting a popular misconception rather than immediately presenting geological data?
2. **(Historical causation)** What kind of outside impression was Hilgard trying to correct, and why might that impression have been so widespread by 1860?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose Hilgard's report had confirmed the popular impression of Mississippi as mostly swamp instead of challenging it. How might that have affected outside interest in settling or investing in the state?
4. **(Conceptual)** What does Hilgard's specific naming of tree types (pine, black jack, post oak) suggest about his approach as a trained scientist compared to a casual traveler's account?

## Primary Source Exercise 2.2 — An 1839 Map of Mississippi

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** What does the number and placement of towns and post roads on this map suggest about where Mississippi's population was concentrated in 1839?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why would post offices and post roads be considered important enough to feature prominently on a state map at this time?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose railroads had already crisscrossed the state by 1839, instead of the small number shown. How might the settlement pattern visible on this map look different?
4. **(Conceptual)** Mississippi's county boundaries kept changing for decades after this map was drawn. What does that suggest about the limits of using a single map as a source for understanding a state's history?

## Discussion

**Prompt:** Compare what Hilgard's 1860 report says about Mississippi's landscape to what the 1839 map shows about where people had settled by that time. Where do the two sources seem to agree with each other, and where might they give you a different picture of the state? Respond to a classmate who focused on a different comparison than you did.

Shared prompt works for both OD and Live sections.

Platform: ENGAGE.

**Title:** Mapping Mississippi's Regions

**What you're doing:** You'll place labeled markers for Mississippi's four major regions on a large floor map or relief model, then compare what an 1860 scientist and an 1839 mapmaker each noticed about the state.

**What you'll need:** VR headset (desktop/mobile fallback available), this week's two lessons and two primary sources already read.

**How to join:** [Session type: temporary] · Session ID: [TBD] · Location: [TBD — a Location with a large floor map or relief-style model of Mississippi; specific Location to be confirmed once available in the ENGAGE library] · Spawn point: at the edge of the floor map.

**What to do, in order:**
1. Using the pre-placed region markers (or a Whiteboard IFX if markers are unavailable), label the approximate locations of the Delta, the Hills, the Piney Woods, and the Gulf Coast on the floor map.
2. Place a sticky note near the Delta region summarizing Hilgard's point about outside misconceptions of Mississippi's landscape.
3. Place a second sticky note near the map's overall layout summarizing what the 1839 map suggests about where Mississippi's population was concentrated.
4. Record a short spatial recording (60–90 seconds) explaining one way the scientist's report and the mapmaker's map tell a similar story, and one way they tell a different one.

**What to submit:** Your spatial recording, submitted through Canvas.

**Time:** Approximately 20 minutes.

**Facilitator note:** Students may want to place markers with GPS-level precision. Remind them this is a conceptual exercise about regions and relationships, not a cartography test. If you're supporting a live section, this is a good moment to ask a student to explain, out loud, why a region's soil type mattered to the people who settled there.

**Success criteria:** Completion-based. The recording correctly places all four regions in roughly the right relative positions and draws a real connection between the two primary sources, not just a restatement of each in isolation.
