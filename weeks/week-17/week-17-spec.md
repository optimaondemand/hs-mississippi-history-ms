## Primary Source Exercise 17.1 — Frederick Law Olmsted, A Journey in the Back Country

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): conceptual, historical causation, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Conceptual)** Olmsted describes planter wealth as "almost miraculous." What does that word choice suggest about how quickly and dramatically this wealth appeared, even to an outside observer?
2. **(Historical causation)** Why might a Northern newspaper have been interested in publishing a detailed travel account of Southern plantation society in the 1850s?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose Olmsted had been a Southern-born planter's son rather than a Northern journalist. How might his descriptions of the same scenes likely have differed?
4. **(Conceptual)** Olmsted records planters weighing "negroes at $1000 a head" against "fresh land in Texas at $1 an acre" in the same economic calculation. What does that comparison reveal about how the planters he spoke with understood the enslaved people they held?

## Primary Source Exercise 17.2 — A Runaway Notice, Southern Telegraph, 1836

4 questions. Types (never shown to students): historical causation, conceptual, counterfactual, conceptual.

1. **(Historical causation)** Why would an enslaver repeat the same advertisement across several months rather than running it only once?
2. **(Conceptual)** This page describes only confirmed details about Peter's advertisement rather than inventing his physical description. Why does that restraint matter especially when the person described was real and named?
3. **(Counterfactual)** Suppose Peter had been recaptured shortly after escaping. How might the historical record of this specific case look different from what has actually survived?
4. **(Conceptual)** A runaway advertisement is written entirely from the enslaver's perspective, yet it reveals something about the enslaved person's own choices. What does that tell you about reading a source against the grain of its author's intent?
