📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Less than two years after Fort Panmure quietly surrendered on paper, a group of the very settlers Britain had recruited to live there took up arms and briefly seized that same fort back from Spain.
Governments changing hands at a negotiating table, as you saw with Galvez's capitulation terms, is only half the story of conquest. The other half is what happens afterward, when a new government actually has to govern the people already living under the old one. This lesson looks at the settlers of the Natchez District in the years just after Spain took control, a population that did not agree among themselves about the change and that included some who were willing to fight it. It also looks at what Spain did, and did not do, to hold the district together once the shooting from the Revolution itself had moved elsewhere.

📚 PART 1 · NOT EVERYONE ACCEPTED THE NEW FLAG

The Natchez Uprising of 1781

The settlers Britain had recruited into the Natchez District over the previous sixteen years, many of them English-speaking planters from Georgia and the Carolinas who had accepted British land grants and remained loyal to the crown, did not automatically transfer that loyalty to Spain simply because a surrender document said the district now belonged to Madrid. In April 1781, a group of these settlers, led by men including a British loyalist named John Blommart, rose up against the small Spanish garrison stationed at Fort Panmure and briefly seized the fort itself, hoping that British forces elsewhere on the Gulf Coast might still relieve them.

That hope did not pan out. By the time the Natchez rebels rose up, Galvez had already captured Mobile and was in the middle of his campaign against Pensacola, the last major British stronghold on the Gulf Coast, and no British relief force was coming. Spanish authorities eventually put down the uprising, and its leaders faced arrest, though Spain's response was notably not to punish the district's population broadly or strip settlers of the land grants Britain had issued them. This particular choice, restraint after a rebellion rather than mass retaliation, is worth noticing, since a harsher response could easily have made the district considerably harder to govern for years afterward.

Blommart himself was captured and eventually sent to Havana as a prisoner, though he later returned to the district and, remarkably, went on to hold minor positions of trust under the very Spanish government he had once taken up arms against. Several other participants faced lesser punishments or none at all once Spanish officials judged the uprising over and the district's overall stability more valuable than making an example of every rebel. That choice, again, reflects a government weighing what actually keeps a mixed population governable against what a stricter reading of the law might technically allow.

The uprising is a useful reminder that a treaty or a capitulation document settles a question on paper far more cleanly than it settles that question in the minds of the people actually living through it. Loyalty to a distant king, or resentment of a new one, does not disappear the moment a flag changes on a pole. Had British forces further south fared even slightly better in 1781, or had reinforcements genuinely been on their way when the Natchez rebels rose up, this brief uprising might have become something considerably larger and Spain's hold on the district might have looked very different in the years that followed.

📚 PART 2 · HOLDING A MIXED DISTRICT TOGETHER

What Spain Actually Had to Govern

By the early 1780s, the population Spain now governed in the Natchez District was genuinely mixed in a way few single colonial governments had managed before: French Catholic families who had lived there since the earlier French period, English-speaking Protestant settlers recruited under British rule, enslaved people brought under both French and British ownership, and Native nations whose land claims predated all three European powers. Spain's own law formally established Catholicism as the district's only recognized religion, which in theory left little room for the Protestant majority Britain had recruited to worship openly.

In practice, Spanish officials in Natchez largely tolerated private Protestant worship as long as it stayed quiet and did not challenge Catholic authority publicly, an informal accommodation that was never guaranteed by law but that Spanish governors judged more practical than strict enforcement. This is a good example of a government solving a real, first-order problem, keeping internal peace among a population that did not share one religion, one language, or one recent loyalty, through flexible practice rather than through the letter of its own official policy. A government that had insisted on rigid enforcement instead might well have provoked far more resistance than the single uprising Natchez actually saw.

Spain also made a deliberate choice to honor land grants and claims that settlers had received under earlier French and British administrations, rather than requiring everyone to reapply from scratch under entirely new Spanish rules. You will see the documentary evidence of this policy directly in this week's second primary source, land and court records that show Spanish officials recognizing claims with origins in a government Spain had just replaced. This continuity mattered enormously to ordinary settlers, whose entire livelihood depended on a stable, recognized claim to their land regardless of which flag happened to be flying over Fort Panmure in a given year.

None of this means Spanish rule solved every problem or satisfied everyone equally. The 1781 uprising shows real limits to how much loyalty Spain actually won, and Spanish control of the Natchez District would itself prove temporary, lasting only until the 1790s, as you will see in the coming weeks. But judged against the basic test this course applies throughout, whether a government manages to keep internal peace and provide enough legal stability for people to build ordinary lives, Spain's mix of religious flexibility and honored land claims did more real work holding the district together than its official policies on paper would suggest by themselves.

This week's virtue thread is self-government, and the Natchez District in these years is a genuinely useful case for thinking about what self-government actually requires before it can exist at all. A political community first needs enough basic order and predictable law that its people can plan their lives without fearing that a rebellion, a land dispute, or a change of flag will upend everything they have built. Spain's practical, sometimes improvised governance of a religiously and legally mixed population was not glamorous and did not resemble any tidy constitutional theory. It was, however, a genuine attempt to solve that first-order problem, and the settlers who eventually pushed for a government more clearly their own, a story the next several weeks will follow all the way to statehood, first needed a district stable enough to make that pursuit possible.
