📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In September 1779, a Spanish governor barely thirty years old sailed up the Mississippi River commanding a fleet a hurricane had already battered, and within three weeks a British fort at Natchez surrendered without a single shot fired inside Mississippi itself.
Mississippi's role in the American Revolution is not the story most students picture when they hear that name, colonial militia facing British regulars on a New England battlefield. It is instead a story about Spain, allied with France against Britain, using the Mississippi River as a corridor of conquest that stretched from New Orleans north into what is now Mississippi. This lesson follows Bernardo de Galvez's Gulf Coast campaign and asks what his success, and the very real chance it might not have happened at all, reveals about how much of this history turned on the decisions of particular people facing particular circumstances, rather than on any outcome that had to occur.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
siege (SEEJ): a military operation in which an army surrounds a fortified place and cuts it off, often bombarding it, in order to force its surrender rather than take it by direct assault.
Gulf Coast campaign (GUHLF kohst kam-PAYN): the series of military operations Bernardo de Galvez led between 1779 and 1781 that captured British forts along the Mississippi River and Gulf of Mexico during the American Revolution.
📚 PART 1 · A HURRICANE, A YOUNG GOVERNOR, AND A DECISION

Spain Enters Britain's War

In June 1779, Spain declared war on Great Britain. Spain's king made this decision as an ally of France, which was already supporting the American colonies, not as a direct ally of the United States itself. Spain had its own reasons for wanting to weaken Britain, including old territorial losses it hoped to reverse and a general interest in seeing British power in North America checked. Whatever Spain's own motives, the effect reached all the way to the Mississippi River, because Spain already controlled Louisiana, and Louisiana's governor was a young, ambitious officer named Bernardo de Galvez, not yet thirty-two years old, who had spent several years building relationships with settlers and Native nations along the lower Mississippi and was eager to act the moment war was formally declared.

Galvez did not wait for detailed instructions from Spain before moving. He had already begun assembling troops, supplies, and boats at New Orleans in anticipation of the declaration, gathering a genuinely mixed force of Spanish regulars, Louisiana militia, free men of color, Native allies, and even a number of American volunteers who saw an opportunity to strike at British holdings from the south. This kind of independent initiative by a distant colonial governor, acting on his own judgment before hearing back from a royal court an ocean away, is itself a reminder that decisions in this period were rarely made by abstract nations so much as by particular people making particular calls, often with incomplete information and real risk to their own careers if they guessed wrong.

Even before Spain formally entered the war, its presence on the Mississippi was already reaching the American cause in a quieter way. A New Orleans merchant named Oliver Pollock, working with Galvez's cooperation, helped move gunpowder and other supplies up the Mississippi and Ohio Rivers to American forces fighting far to the north, including the campaign George Rogers Clark led against British posts in what is now Illinois and Indiana. None of this aid depended on any grand strategy handed down from a single capital. It depended on particular relationships between particular men, a Spanish governor willing to look the other way and a merchant willing to take the financial risk, long before Spain's king ever signed a declaration of war.

Then, in August 1779, disaster struck before Galvez's campaign had even begun: a hurricane tore through New Orleans and wrecked much of the fleet he had assembled, sinking or damaging supply ships and scattering the resources he had spent months collecting. A more cautious commander might reasonably have delayed the entire campaign for months to rebuild, giving British forces along the Mississippi valuable time to reinforce their own defenses. Galvez instead chose to press forward with what he had left, a decision that was anything but guaranteed to succeed and that historians still treat as a turning point in whether this campaign happened at all in 1779, rather than a year or more later under very different circumstances.

📚 PART 2 · THE FALL OF FORT PANMURE

A Campaign That Reached Natchez Without a Battle There

Galvez's depleted force moved north from New Orleans in early September 1779 and captured its first target, a modest British post called Fort Bute near Manchac, within days and with little resistance. The larger test came next at Baton Rouge, a well-built British fort protected by wide ditches, earthen walls, and more than a dozen cannons. Rather than attacking the fort's strongest point directly, Galvez used a diversion, positioning some of his troops to draw British fire and attention away from where he actually placed his artillery, and after several hours of bombardment the fort's defenses were badly damaged and its commander, Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Dickson, requested terms.

What happened next is the detail that connects this campaign directly to Mississippi. Galvez's terms of capitulation, the formal document spelling out what Dickson's surrender would actually cover, required the British to give up not only Baton Rouge but also Fort Panmure at Natchez, a separate post more than a hundred miles upriver that Galvez had never attacked and whose garrison had faced no fighting at all. Dickson agreed, and Fort Panmure changed hands purely on paper, decided at a negotiating table outside Baton Rouge by two commanders working out the terms of somebody else's battle.

Judged by the standard this course has used throughout, whether a leader's own character helps explain success or failure at a real, first-order problem, Galvez's insistence on including Natchez in the surrender terms is worth taking seriously as a case of genuine boldness paying off. A more timid or purely by-the-book commander, one who negotiated only for what he had actually besieged, might have left Fort Panmure standing for the British to reinforce later. Galvez's decisiveness at the negotiating table, not just on the battlefield, is a real part of why an entire district changed flags without a fight ever reaching it.

Within about three weeks, Spain had removed British military power from the entire lower Mississippi corridor, a remarkably fast result that depended on several things that did not have to go Spain's way: a hurricane that could have wrecked the campaign entirely rather than merely delaying it, a British commander willing to include a fort he had not lost militarily, and a young governor willing to gamble on speed over caution. None of this was settled in advance. Had any one of those pieces gone differently, a slower Spanish rebuild, a more stubborn Dickson, a more cautious Galvez, Fort Panmure at Natchez might well have remained in British hands for years longer, and the district's later history under Spain, which the rest of this week studies in detail, would not have unfolded the same way at all. Galvez would go on to lead further campaigns against Mobile in 1780 and Pensacola in 1781, eventually driving British power out of the entire Gulf Coast, but it began here, with a decision made in the ruins of a hurricane.
